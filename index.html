Scientists Recommend Having Fun With Your Girlfriend Among Top Activities, Study Finds

Researchers say the surprisingly effective practice may have been overlooked by previous generations of scientists.

By The International Scientific Herald Science Desk   ·   Aduongeow, Naoero

Scientists have concluded that having fun with one's girlfriend is among the activities most strongly associated with positive outcomes in everyday life, according to a new study published Tuesday by the Vesperian Institute for Applied Happiness.

The study, conducted over a period of 14 months, examined the recreational habits of 3,842 participants across six countries and one location that researchers declined to identify.

Participants were asked to describe activities they considered enjoyable and then indicate whether those activities made them feel “good,” “very good,” or “quite good, actually.”

“We initially expected the results to be considerably more complicated. Instead, the data kept pointing toward people enjoying themselves.”

— Dr. Elias Vorn, lead researcher

The researchers said the finding remained statistically significant after accounting for age, weather, weekday, time of day and whether participants had recently eaten.

Among the activities examined were watching films, going for walks, talking, laughing, visiting restaurants, playing games and “doing something neither participant had specifically planned.”

The study found that participants who regularly engaged in enjoyable activities with someone they liked reported higher levels of satisfaction with their leisure time.

The researchers acknowledged that this conclusion may appear obvious.

“That was one of the more interesting methodological challenges. We had to make sure the result wasn't simply obvious.”

To address this concern, the team performed the analysis again. The result was essentially unchanged.

A Long-Overlooked Variable

Researchers said previous studies of happiness have focused heavily on sleep, exercise, social connection and financial security, while comparatively little attention has been paid to the more general question of whether people are, in fact, having a good time.

“This represents a potentially important gap in the literature,” said Prof. Miriam Salk, who was not involved in the study.

The researchers emphasized that their findings should not be interpreted as a universal prescription. People should continue doing activities they personally enjoy, the report states. The evidence merely suggests that enjoying those activities may be beneficial.

The institute plans to conduct a follow-up study examining whether having even more fun produces proportionally greater benefits.

Funding for the next phase has not yet been secured.

Researchers said they remain optimistic.

“We have several promising leads.”

One of them, he added, is “going out and doing something fun.”
